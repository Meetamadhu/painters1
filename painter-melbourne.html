Painter Melbourne — Venetian plaster, limewash, Tadelakt,
microcement, metallic and clay for residential interiors

Move the light and watch what the surface does with it. Flat paint returns one reflection all day. A hand-applied mineral finish scatters light through depth — cool and chalky at seven, bleached at midday, burning along the trowel marks when the sun drops. That difference is the whole reason these finishes cost what they cost.

One room at a time, so the house stays usable. Around three to four times the cost of a standard repaint, and it does not need redoing in seven years.

Act 02 — The light test

Two walls. One window. Only one of them is doing anything.

Both surfaces below are the same colour. Drag the light through a full day and the acrylic holds its single note while the plaster opens, clouds and burnishes. Nothing has been added to the room — only the hour has changed.

Acrylic sits as a sealed film with one reflection angle. Mineral finishes are porous and slightly irregular, so light enters, scatters and leaves at a different angle from every square centimetre. That is also why they breathe, why they suit solid brick, and why a bad substrate shows through instantly.

Flat acrylic, low sheen One reflection angle. One mood, from breakfast to bedtime. 2 coats over sealer. 4 hours recoat. Wipeable. 7–10 year repaint cycle.
Burnished Venetian plaster Depth you can read from across the room, and again at 9pm. 6–8 coats. 21 day cure. Wax-sealed, wipeable. Repairable in place.
Act 03 — Specimen index

Six ways to stop a wall being just a wall.

Venetian plaster · Specimen 001

Lime putty and marble dust, laid in six to eight passes and burnished back to a stone-like sheen. Reads almost flat at midday, then opens into visible depth as the light drops. The one to choose when a room needs to feel older than it is.

Needs a flat, sound substrate — any hollow or patch telegraphs through. Wax-sealed after burnishing, so it wipes clean and can be repaired in place rather than recoated. Not for wet walls in a shower.

Act 04 — Before and after
Before · flat acrylic After · Venetian 001

Conceptual surface study — both sides lit by the same rail. Real before-and-after photographs drop into these two layers.

The same wall, before and after, at whatever hour you like.

Drag the divider across. Then move the light rail and drag it again. A before-and-after fixed at one flattering moment tells you very little — the question is whether the change still holds at seven in the morning, and at nine at night.

The left side is a typical Melbourne interior wall: low-sheen acrylic over two previous colours, a patched hook line, roller laps you can pick out in raking light, and scuffing along the skirting. Everything visible on that side has to be made flat before a mineral finish goes anywhere near it.

Act 05 — Choosing colour

The paint chip lied to you. Here is exactly how.

One colour, four hours. Nothing about the paint has changed — only the light reaching it. This is why a sample the size of a business card, held up under showroom fluorescents, tells you almost nothing about your own hallway.

07:10 — south facing

Blue cast off open sky. Warm colours go muddy and everything reads a shade darker.

12:40 — direct sun

Bleaching. Undertones vanish and two very different colours can look identical.

16:20 — low west sun

The hour that sells the job. Texture casts micro-shadow and depth appears.

20:50 — lamps only

2700K globes push everything warm. This is when you actually live in the room.
